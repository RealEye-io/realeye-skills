# From eye tracking to a creative decision

> **Report rule:** the HTML report uses only VAI and its components (Reach, Speed, Hold). Raw metrics mentioned below (fixation ratio, TTFF, fixation time, counts) explain RealEye exports and must not appear in the report.


## Questions a brand team needs answered

Define what earns attention for a reason. The strongest visual need not be the best-performing brand asset; the useful question is whether it directs enough attention to what the creative is meant to communicate.

| Decision question | Evidence to examine | A change worth testing |
|---|---|---|
| **What catches the eye first?** Does the opening focal point suit a quick glance? | Early fixation Ratio plus TTFF among fixators for hero, brand and key copy. Use observed order to test a specific sequence. A central first look may reflect the starting position rather than the image's design. | Keep an effective hero. If eyes rarely progress from it to the message, test placing the message nearer the hero or reducing one competing visual, not both at once. |
| **Does the brand get credit?** Is the logo, pack brand, or distinctive asset reached in time? | Early and longer-window Ratio on verified brand AOIs, paired with the hero's results. Is the brand late, or missed even with more time? Separate the pack from its much smaller label only if tracking can resolve both. | Test moving the brand cue toward a well-viewed product/claim or making it more distinct. Separately test unaided brand recall or recognition; visual contact is not attribution. |
| **Is the offer or key claim easy to find?** | Ratio, TTFF, and total fixation time among those who reached the headline, benefit, price, or claim. High reach with short looks could be quick pickup or superficial exposure. Long looking could be reading or effort. | Low reach: test contrast, whitespace, or placement. High reach but weak message takeout on a separate question: test simplifying the claim. Use open-ended message takeout before prompted checks. |
| **Is there a clear next step?** | Ratio and timing for the intended CTA, with order or transitions if supported. Use click/choice only when measured with a relevant task. | Make the action distinct or nearer the offer. Test a real click or choose task; viewing a CTA on a static mockup is not taking action. |
| **Competing information or a useful cue?** | Fixations on faces, badges, secondary offers, legal copy, or decorations alongside the primary AOIs. A hotspot is not wasted attention just because it is not the CTA. | Reduce one nonessential competitor only if the primary element is underserved. Preserve required text and useful cues; for important fine print, use a separate legibility/comprehension check. |

## Interpret patterns with the study conditions attached

- **Free viewing vs. directed search.** Free viewing helps diagnose what attracts attention unprompted under this setup. A task like "find the price" is evidence about findability under that instruction, not spontaneous price salience. Do not generalize a five-second isolated image to a one-second feed impression, a real shelf, a click, or a purchase.
- **First look vs. breadth vs. depth.** Early Ratio and first fixation order answer different questions. Similar TTFF among fixators can hide very different miss rates. High cumulative reach after a longer exposure is not evidence of early reach. When nested windows genuinely track the same people, the rise in reach shows how many *additional* viewers fixated by the later cutoff, not how long they looked.
- **Size, position and measurement.** Larger/central AOIs cover more opportunities for fixations. A tiny logo or crowded label may be below practical tracking resolution. Inspect boundaries and placement, use visually comparable AOIs across variants, and check if a reasonable change to a boundary would change the recommendation. Do not equate an AOI's rank or per-area normalization with causal salience. When early central bias might drive the conclusion, compare matched windows with and without the first 0.5 s if those data are available.
- **Watch the base.** A pattern in 3 of 4 people is not as stable as the same proportion in a large sample. Report group sizes and selection, distinguish planned comparisons from exploratory cuts, and avoid declaring segment differences from small groups. Missing gaze, unequal coverage, and different device mixes can mimic a design effect. A repeated hotspot can also hide distinct paths across viewers.

## Retest without overclaiming

A single version diagnoses *where to investigate*, not what caused an effect. For a useful comparison, use equivalent semantic regions and matched image format, screen/device, instructions, viewing duration, time origin, filters, and fixation settings. If shape or location changes by design, document the new geometry. Randomly assign people to versions for independent first impressions; for a within-person design, counterbalance order, acknowledge repeat exposure and analyze paired outcomes. Avoid formal significance or causal claims from aggregate tables, unmatched cohorts or heatmap colors. If inference is required, use participant-level data and uncertainty at the correct independent or paired unit.

Before a retest, name one deciding outcome, a meaningful change, and a guardrail. For example: *increase correct unaided recall of both brand and claim; check 0.5-3 s claim and logo Ratio to see if the placement change affected visual access.* There is no universal threshold for good Ratio, TTFF, dwell, or VAI. Use a matched tested baseline, not another brand's published number.
